@ Stephen 
Training the n-gram and LSTM models on random rollouts from the environment will unfortunately cap their performance at “random,” which makes comparison against the RL models less meaningful. The point of the baselines is that it should be purely data-driven approach to melody modelling.
Here are a couple better approaches for collecting data that will give your baselines something real to learn:
1. Use a small curated corpus of symbolic melodies (recommended)
Pick a handful of 4-bar monophonic MIDI melodies in a fixed key (C major or minor). Magenta, Nottingham Dataset, or even a few hand-picked folk melodies work fine.
Convert to note sequences (pitch, duration).
Train the n-gram and LSTM models to model these sequences.
This gives them a meaningful statistical structure to learn — scale conformity, rhythmic motifs, repetition, etc.
This is the typical way n-gram/LSTM baselines are used in music-generation research.

2. Use heuristic-generated melodies, but not random
If you prefer synthetic data, generate sequences using your simple heuristic rules:
stay in scale
prefer stepwise motion
choose durations from a small rhythmic vocabulary
This produces “sensible but simple” melodies that are still learnable.

You’ll then be comparing:
Data-driven learners (n-gram, LSTM) trained on a real distribution vs RL agents that start from scratch but get better via your reward shaping
This makes your evaluation meaningful. 
TL;DR
Random-environment data isn’t sufficient. Give the baselines structured melodic data — either from a small MIDI corpus or a heuristic generator; so they can actually model something.
