\section{Introduction}

This project focuses on training a Reinforcement Learning (RL) agent to generate musical compositions, of predefined lengths typically 4 to 16 bars, from scratch. Our agent learns purely through trial and error without supervised pre-training as is often used in existing approaches.
The core challenge lies in creating musically coherent sequences with the reward as the sole guide. Musical quality is reliant upon multiple dimensions, such as rhythm, repetition, melody, harmony, and structural progression. Additionally, music generation differs from standard RL applications because the quality of a composition relies upon the generation of each note and is heavily dependent on its interaction with both previous and subsequent notes. This requires the agent to handle multiple competing objectives simultaneously.

This problem is significant for several reasons. In reinforcement learning, music generation serves as an excellent testbed for sequential decision making in action spaces with delayed and fine rewards. Musical quality is very multidimensional and subjective, as it often requires careful engineering for the rewards. This is different when we take a look at typical RL benchmarks with success metrics that are clear, such as game scores. 

Understanding how an RL agent can learn musicality solely from environmental interaction and reward feedback provides insights into a lesser-studied method of artificial music composition.
It allows for direct comparison of this technique against more well-established traditional methods, such as supervised and unsupervised learning with deep neural networks.
Exploring the capabilities of RL agents in composing meaningful musical sequences is also transferable to studying RL methods. It allows analyzing long-term dependency and self-referential problems, which are impactful in real-world planning and control systems.

We frame the generation process as a constrained Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$:

\begin{description}
  \item[State Space ($\mathcal{S}$):] The state represents the musical composition as it is generated. It is internally represented as a dynamic array of bars. As the agent generates notes, they are appeneded to the current bar. The internal representation is converted to an fixed length array of zeros with the generated notes represented in respective indices. To assist the agent in subsequent note generation the number of beats remaining the the current bar is appended to this flattened observation and both are returned to the agent.

  \item[Action Space ($\mathcal{A}$):] The action space is discrete, consisting of 90 total actions. These represent the constrained set of notes spanning two octaves of the C major scale (MIDI pitch 60 to 84), combined with specific note durations (sixteenth, eighth, quarter, half, or whole) and rests of corresponding lengths.

  \item[Transition Function ($P$):] The state transitions are deterministic. When an action is selected, the chosen note is appended to the internal score. If a selected note duration exceeds the remaining beats of the current bar, it is truncated to the remaining beats and penalized. The current beat counter is updated and bars are added to the internal representation as appropriate. The episode terminates when the score reaches the pre-defined number of bars.

  \item[Reward Function ($R$):] We employ a sparse reward structure. The agent receives immediate negative penalties for invalid actions. The reward for a composition is calculated at the terminal state. It is mathematical evaluation of rhythmic quality, harmonic and melodic structure, progression, and repetition in addition to penalties for violation of the above musical properties.

  \item[Discount Factor ($\gamma$):] Due to the compositional nature of musical composition, we do not discount future rewards, setting $\gamma = 1$.
\end{description}

\section{Approaches}

\subsection{Prior Works}

Traditional methods of music generation often involve supervised learning and deep neural networks like Long short-term memory (LSTM) models \citep{lstm-paper}. \citet{RNN-finetune} applied Deep Q-RL \citep{dqn-paper} finetuning to an LSTM with a reward function derived from music theory heuristics (rhythm, harmony, etc.). Their goal was to circumvent the traditional purely data driven approach and generate monophonic melodies. Similarly, in \textit{Bach2Bach}, \citet{bach2bach} employ a bi-axial LSTM as an initial probabilistic model to which they apply a Deep Q-Learning Network (DQN) RL approach to improve quality.

A more recent approch discussed by \citet{music-rlhf} employs reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \citep{rlhf} to a text-to-music autoregressive model. This powerful approach demonstrated significant improvements over their baseline models. \textit{Music Generation using Human-In-The-Loop Reinforcement Learning} \citep{hitlp-paper} is another very recent paper in which the the reseachers apply RLHF to learn music generation. They implement a tabular Q-learning \citep{q-learning} approach in which human raters provide feedback per generation guiding their model to learn subjective human preferences.

Important to many of the RL approaches is designing a comprehensive and useful reward function. \citet{rhythm-paper} describe a number of methods for computing mathematical descriptions of the desireability of rhythm, repetition, motif, and transitions among other components in music. They quantify global desireability of a piece from local properties such as note timings, onsets, duration, pattern repetitions.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Agents}

Our primary focus lies in training RL agents from scratch to generate musical compositions purely through environmental interaction. We employ the Stable-Baselines3 library~\citep{stable-baselines3} to implement two state-of-the-art RL algorithms.

\subsubsection{Deep Q-Network (DQN)}

DQN is a value-based off-policy algorithm that learns to approximate the action-value function $Q(s, a)$, representing the expected return when taking action $a$ in state $s$ and following the optimal policy thereafter \citep{dqn-paper}. The algorithm maintains two key components: a replay buffer storing past experiences $(s, a, r, s')$ and a target network to stabilize training. The Q-network is updated by minimizing the temporal difference error:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s') \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\left(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\text{target}}(s', a') - Q(s, a; \theta)\right)^2\right]
\]

For our music generation task, we configure DQN with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$, replay buffer size of $10^6$, and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration that anneals from 1.0 to 0.05 over 15\% of training. The agent learns through 20,000 episodes of 8-bar composition generation, with experience replay enabling efficient learning from past compositions. The target network updates every 8,000 steps to maintain training stability. This off-policy approach allows the agent to learn from suboptimal compositions while gradually improving its policy.

\subsubsection{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)}

PPO is an on-policy actor-critic algorithm that directly learns a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ while estimating a value function $V(s)$ \citep{ppo-paper}. Unlike DQN, PPO updates the policy using the clipped surrogate objective to prevent destructively large policy updates:
\[
\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]
\]

where $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}$ is the probability ratio and $\hat{A}_t$ is the advantage estimate computed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) with $\lambda = 0.95$.

We configure PPO with a learning rate of $3 \times 10^{-4}$, collecting 1,024 timesteps per update, and performing 10 epochs of minibatch updates with batch size 256. The clipping range is set to 0.2, and we use an entropy coefficient of 0.015 to encourage exploration. PPO trains for 10,000 episodes, directly optimizing the policy to maximize expected compositional quality. The on-policy nature ensures the agent learns from its current policy's behavior, potentially leading to more stable convergence for sequential decision-making in music generation.
Both RL agents use a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) policy network that processes the flattened observation vector (musical score representation plus remaining beats). The agents receive no musical training data and learn purely from the reward signal defined by our multi-faceted reward function.

\subsection{Baseline Methods}

To contextualize the performance of our RL agents, we implement two supervised learning baselines trained on musical data.

\subsubsection{N-gram Language Model}

We adapt the n-gram language modeling approach to musical sequence generation using NLTK's implementation. The model treats musical actions as tokens and learns conditional probability distributions $P(a_t | a_{t-n+1}, \ldots, a_{t-1})$ from a corpus of collected compositions \citep{ngram-site}.
We use a bigram model ($n=2$) with Laplace smoothing to handle unseen action sequences. The vocabulary consists of all 90 possible actions from our discrete action space plus start and end tokens. Training data comprises rollouts collected from both random and trained RL agents, stored as sequences of action indices.
During generation, the model samples the next action based on the learned conditional probabilities, constrained to select only valid actions given the remaining beats in the current bar. When the model encounters contexts with no learned transitions, it falls back to uniform sampling over valid actions. This approach provides a probabilistic baseline that captures local sequential patterns in musical composition.

\subsubsection{LSTM Neural Network}

Our LSTM baseline employs a recurrent neural network to model longer-term dependencies in musical sequences \citep{lstm-paper}. The architecture consists of an embedding layer mapping action indices to dense vectors (128 dimensions), a single-layer LSTM with 256 hidden units and dropout (0.3), followed by a fully-connected output layer producing logits over the action vocabulary.
The model is trained on the same action sequence dataset using teacher forcing, where at each timestep the model predicts the next action given the previous ground-truth actions. We use cross-entropy loss with padding token masking:
\[
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(a_t | a_{1:t-1}; \theta)
\]

Due to the variable and often lengthy compositions in the Nottingham MIDI dataset, we chunk sequences into overlapping segments of 64 actions to facilitate efficient training. The model trains for 20 epochs using Adam optimizer with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, saving the best model based on validation loss.
For generation, the LSTM autoregressively samples actions, maintaining hidden state across the composition. Like the n-gram model, action selection is constrained to valid durations given bar boundaries. The LSTM's recurrent architecture enables it to capture longer melodic and rhythmic patterns compared to the Markov assumption of n-grams.

\subsection{Reward Function Design}

The reward function serves as the sole learning signal for our RL agents and plays a critical role in shaping compositional quality. We design a multi-component reward function that evaluates four fundamental aspects of musical structure: rhythmic coherence, harmonic correctness, functional chord progression, and repetition of notes and motifs. Each element produces a normalized score in $[0,1]$. After combining these components with their respectful weights, we subtract several global penalties designed to prevent musically nonsensical behavior. This results in a final scalar reward our agent attempts to maximize. 

\subsubsection{Rhythm Reward} The rhythm reward evaluates temporal structure across the entire sequence. For each note, we convert its onset into a discrete subdivision grid (four subdivisions per quarter note). This representation allows rhythmic structure to be evaluated in a meaningful way, because variations in tempo, expressive timing, as well as bar length all become comparable once the note onsets are expressed as relative positions within this grid. There are five metrics that contribute to the Rhythm Reward:

\paragraph{Quarter-beat ratio:}
We compute the fraction of notes beginning on strong beats by dividing the number of onsets divisible by 4 over the total number of onsets. We do this to maintain structure while still allowing syncopation. Notes starting on strong beats give the melody a rhythmic grounding, and deviations from the beat allow for more tension.

\paragraph{Inter-onset interval stability (rhythmic regularity):}
The inter-onset interval (IOI) is the time difference between the start of one note and the next, which is an important measure of spacing as well as temporal regularity.
Given onset times $\{t_i\}$, we compute IOI variance to measure regularity:
\vspace{-4pt}
\[
s_{\text{IOI}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{Var}(t_{i+1} - t_i)}{(\mathbb{E}[t_{i+1}-t_i])^2 + \varepsilon}
\]
Stable IOIs correlate to rhythmic pacing that is predictable, which is a common property of melodies made by humans, while still allowing intentional deviations.

\paragraph{Entropy:}
To prevent monotonous or rhythms that are overly chaotic, we compute the entropy of note 
placements within a bar. A moderate entropy value corresponds to the level of rhythmic variety 
typically seen in simple melodies, whereas very low entropy signals repetition, and high 
entropy signals rhythmic noise.

\paragraph{Note density:}
Melodies must avoid less desirable behaviors such as emitting too many notes (high density) or too few 
(low density). We therefore reward note counts per bar that fall near an expected range, to stabilize learning and prevent spare or overly dense rhythms. 

\paragraph{Syncopation:}
The goal is to achieve moderate syncopation, as it is expressive, and encourages off-beat emphasis when it is appropriate to do so, without producing unpredictable accents. It allows more expression in the sequence. To achieve this, we reward deviations from the beat up to a target rate of 20\%.

All five metrics are averaged using empirically tuned weights $(1.8, 1.2, 0.9, 0.8, 1.0)$ to yield the final rhythm score, the fraction of sum of each metric score multiplied by weight over sum of weights.

\subsubsection{Harmony Reward}

To evaluate correctness of the harmony, we assign each bar a chord label using a rule-based detector that 
scores candidate chords according to three factors: Pitch content, beat salience, and note duration weighting. Given the detected chord for a bar, each note contributes to the harmony score as follows~\citep{piston2011harmony}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chord tones} receive a reward of $+2.0$, as these tones establish the harmonic identity of the bar and are important to stabilizing the tone.
    \item \textbf{Diatonic non-chord tones (NCTs)} are classified using intervallic relationships 
    and receive fractional credit depending on melodic function:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Passing tones, neighbor tones, escape tones: $+0.5$
        \item Appoggiaturas: $+0.75$
        \item Anticipations: $+0.6$
        \item Suspensions: $+0.75$, retardations: $+0.65$
        \item Pedal tones: $+0.6$
    \end{itemize}
Rewarding NCTs prevents the agent from overfitting to melodies that are completely chordal, resulting in more expressive and human-like melodic patterns.
\end{itemize}

These categories align with music theory and are detected using previous and next notes as pitch context. To prevent chord-heavy bars from negatively influencing the proportions of the total reward, we calculate our total harmony reward as the following:

Let $S_{\text{harmony}}$ denote the sum of all per-note contributions. We normalize using an ideal maximum per-note value of $1.7$:
\vspace{-4pt}
\[
R_{\text{harmony}} = \frac{1}{1.7} \cdot \frac{S_{\text{harmony}}}{\#\text{melodic notes}}
\]

\subsubsection{Progression Reward}

Harmonies that are of basic correctness are not sufficient enough for generating structured and coherent music, 
so the progression reward evaluates how well adjacent bars follow harmony motion in appropriate style~\citep{kostka2017tonal}. \\ The process is as follows: Detect the chord in each bar $b$: $C_b$, map each chord to a functional root to normalize chord qualities, and then finally look up the transition weight $w(C_b \rightarrow C_{b+1})$ describing how strong that motion is.

The final progression score averages these transition weights:
\vspace{-4pt}
\[
R_{\text{progression}} =
\frac{1}{N_{\text{valid}}}
\sum_{b} w(C_b \rightarrow C_{b+1})
\]
where $N_{\text{valid}}$ is the number of valid bar pairs. A valid bar pair means that they have detected chords in the pair. Stronger transitions receive higher weights, meaning there is smooth and coherent movement across bars.

\subsubsection{Repetition Reward}

To structure motifs, we flatten the melody into a sequence of note tokens shaped as $(\text{pitch}, \text{duration})$ and compute $n$-gram motifs for $n \in \{2,3,4\}$. N-grams naturally model short melodic pieces which allow repetition to be detected without requiring complicated segmentation or boundaries. We choose from 2 to 4 as 2 signifies uncommon motifs, and 3-4 note motifs are more common in music theory. For each $n$, we slide an $n$-length window over the melody, and then extract each appropriate $n$-gram as pitch-duration pairs. Finally, we count occurrences of each motif. There are 3 external factors that are taken into account:

\paragraph{Meaningful repetition:} Motifs repeated 2–3 times contribute positively, while excessive repetition ($\ge 4$) provides penalties. This is because human melodies rely on repetition for memorability, but excessive looping results in monotony or the agent cheating the reward.

\paragraph{Variety bonus:} We reward the presence of multiple distinct motifs that repeat multiple times:
\vspace{-4pt}
\[
r_{\text{variety}} = \min \left( 0.3 \cdot \frac{|M_{\text{rep}}|}{|M_{\text{all}}|},\, 0.2 \right)
\]
This is rewarded as it encourages diversity of motifs rather than simple pattern copying, which helps to augment the song and make it more complex.

\paragraph{Novelty bonus:} Unique motifs receive an additional bonus. This balances repetition of motifs with creativity that draws interest to the song, rewarding melodies that introduce new ideas without becoming too chaotic.

The final repetition score is capped at $0.75$:
\vspace{-4pt}
\[
R_{\text{repetition}} = \min(r_{\text{motif}} + r_{\text{variety}} + 0.5 r_{\text{novelty}},\, 0.75)
\]

\subsubsection{Penalties}

Our training revealed several faults including things like long-note exploitation, empty bars, 
and mechanical rhythms. Due to these faults, we decided to introduce auxiliary penalties:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bar monotony penalty}: 
    penalizes bars whose durations are uniform. Without this penalty, the agent frequently converged to trivial solutions using repeated quarter notes.
    \item \textbf{Early bar quality}: 
    discourages empty or overly sparse openings, which prevents melodies that begin with long rests or single whole notes.
    \item \textbf{Long-note exploitation}: 
    penalizes excessive use of whole notes or consecutive long durations. Implemented as agents quickly learned that long notes maximize harmony score unless penalized.
    \item \textbf{Duration variety}: 
    encourages multiple rhythmic values.
    \item \textbf{Structural penalties}: 
    penalize weak endings and empty mid-piece bars.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Final Combination}

All reward component scores are normalized to $[0,1]$ and combined as:\\
\vspace{-4pt}
\[
R_{\text{base}} = 
0.45\,R_{\text{rhythm}} +
0.22\,R_{\text{harmony}} +
0.18\,R_{\text{progression}} +
0.15\,R_{\text{repetition}}
\]
\[
R_{\text{final}} = \max\left( 0,\, R_{\text{base}} - \text{TotalPenalty} \right)
\]

The weights were selected specifically to reflect the importance of each musical dimension relative to each other, with rhythm emphasized due to its strong influence on how music is perceived.

This design reflects a broader challenge in applying RL to a domain that relies on creativity rather than simple logic. Having to formalize subjective musical criteria into objectives that we could quantify and have remain learnable was difficult to formulate. Our approach to our reward balances the structure of music theory with elements on tuning, to receive coherent and consistent compositions in terms of melody, harmony, rhythm, and expression.

\section{Empirical Studies}

For each algorithm, we essentially trained multiple agents with different random seeds to assess their consistency. So, we tracked episode rewards throughout the training process and then evaluated the final policies on a test set of generated compositions. We compared four approaches through well-designed experiments (DQN agent, PPO agent, N-gram Baseline, and LSTM Baseline). 

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
For our performance metrics, the primary evaluation metric was the average reward achieved on generated compositions. This allowed the reward to encompass all aspects of musical quality as defined by our reward function. 
Secondary metrics included: 
(1) Individual reward component scores (rhythm, harmony, progression, and  repetition)
(2) Learning stability (variance in episode rewards)
(3) Convergence speed (steps to reach threshold performance)
(4) Musical diversity (variety in generated compositions)

Looking at Figure~\ref{note_distribution} we can see that when running with 3 distinct seeds, the note distribution changes each time. This results in different music being generated each time. While the note distribution changes, the duration does not, so it seems like the agent has found the optimal durations to used, but not quite for the pitches.

As seen in Table~\ref{tab:ppo_ablation}, we tested different configurations of the hyperparameters for the PPO agent, to see how they would impact the final reward and general music structure of the compositions.

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Method Outcomes}

We have had some success in designing and environment and reward function capable of a guiding RL agents to learn a policy for the generation of constrained musical compositions. Our PPO agent succeeds in learning a high reward policy however the quality of the compositions as evaluated by human listeners is still not very high. DQN fails entirely to learn, and comparing against supervised baselines LSTSM generation was as successful and pleasing to humans if not exceeding our RL methods.

We have discovered that the final quality relies heavily upon the reward function and as such its design is crucial to the outcome. It is very challenging to completely capture and describe ideal musical structure across its many dimensions in a purely mathematical formula.

\subsection{Future Recommendations}

We recommend future research perform a deeper investigation of the possibility of expressing musical quality mathematically and to focus on actor-critic algorithms like PPO over DQN and other off-policy algorithms.

We believe it may be of value to focus reward formulas as particular dimensions of musicality, harmony, rhythm, etc. rather than attempting to represent them all in one reward.

Additionally we are interested in exploring the application of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (Citations) to music generation, and its use in improving existing supervised and unsupervised learning methods as demonstrated in our baselines and widely supported in existing literature.
